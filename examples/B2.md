## What applies, and which rule governs

| Regime | Does it apply? | What it means for you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EU GDPR | Yes, at least through your customers | Your Dutch and German forwarders are controllers. I have assumed you are their processor. They need a processing agreement with you and a lawful way to send data to Singapore and on to the US. |
| Singapore PDPA | Yes | For customer documents you act as a data intermediary: security, retention limits, and prompt breach notice to the customer. For your own data the full obligations apply, including appointing a data protection officer and the overseas-transfer rule. |
| EU AI Act | Yes, as a provider | It reaches providers putting AI systems on the EU market wherever they are based. On my proposed classification the duties are light. |
| US law | Only as a transfer destination | Your hosting and model are in the US, so each transfer needs a legal tool. |

Where the regimes differ, GDPR is stricter on the points that matter (it has no carve-out for business contact details, and it sets tighter breach and transfer rules). I propose one baseline at the GDPR standard for all customers, with the Singapore-specific items on top. The decision log held only finance entries, none of which constrain this.

## The five things that matter most

1. **A data processing agreement with each EU pilot.** If the pilots started without one, this is the first gap to close. I will draft the template through `legal-commercial-contracts` once you confirm.
2. **A transfer mechanism for EU to Singapore.** Standard contractual clauses are the usual tool. I am not aware of an EU adequacy decision for Singapore; your lawyer should confirm on the Commission's list.
3. **Your US providers' terms.** You need their data processing agreements, whether they retain prompts or train on your data, and whether they are certified under the EU-US Data Privacy Framework. The EU General Court upheld that framework on 3 September 2025 and an appeal was lodged on 31 October 2025 (WilmerHale, 1 Dec 2025). I have not checked the appeal's status since then, so treat the framework as usable but not guaranteed.
4. **Whether GDPR binds you directly.** This decides whether you need an EU representative. It is a legal judgement I am not making for you; it is question 1 for EU counsel.
5. **AI Act classification.** See below.

## Proposed AI classification (your approval needed)

- **Role:** provider of an AI system; your customers are deployers. You use a third-party general-purpose model and are not a provider of one, assuming you do not fine-tune and redistribute it.
- **Proposed class: not high-risk.** The system checks commercial documents for private forwarders. It is not used by customs or border authorities, does not evaluate individuals, and is not a safety component. This changes if an authority uses it or if it scores named people.
- **What still applies:** AI literacy measures for people operating the system, in the form relaxed this year (measures and documentation, no proof of individual competence). Transparency duties under Article 50 may apply depending on your interface; they have applied since 2 August 2026, with machine-readable marking for systems already on the market due by 2 December 2026.
- **What was postponed:** stand-alone high-risk obligations moved to 2 December 2027 and product-embedded ones to 2 August 2028. These matter only if the classification changes.
- Source: Lewis Silkin, 27 July 2026, reporting the Digital Omnibus on AI published 24 July 2026 and in force 27 July 2026. This is a secondary source; check the Official Journal text.
